I spent roughly five years as a software and QA automation engineer in Kathmandu, Nepal. The turning point came during the COVID-19 pandemic. My mother had started a small organic composting farm, and when it struggled, I built her an e-commerce platform, the kind of thing I had done many times before. It failed, not because the code was broken, but because I did not understand the people side of it: why farmers like my mother would or would not adopt a new tool, how trust in a platform forms, what moves someone from curiosity to action. I could not troubleshoot a social problem the way I could a broken build. That gap, between what I could build and what I could not explain, was the beginning of this shift.

It led me to the Open Institute for Social Science in Kathmandu (2021–2022), where I completed a postgraduate diploma in research and writing and focused my work on digital adoption and the perceptions of Nepali farmers. Working across ethnographic and quantitative research has been deeply rewarding, even when it creates friction with the habits of my engineering training. Over time, I have become more convinced that engineering, statistics, and data science are only one way of observing society, and one way of telling the stories that matter. Computational social science reinforced that view: it is a powerful way to ask different kinds of questions at scale. If anything, the shift made one difference especially clear: in social science, the question comes first and the method follows, not the other way around. My engineering background is still useful, but now in a narrower way, helping with data collection, scraping infrastructure, and reproducible pipelines, the work that supports research without being the research itself.

At the [Hertie School](https://www.hertie-school.org/) (MSc Data Science for Public Policy, 2023–2025, [Data for Good scholarship](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nxFAZHvmvKWNcBQa8c_GPIWX29dQqGdH/view?usp=sharing)), I moved further from building systems and closer to asking questions about politics and platforms. My thesis on political TikTok virality in Nepal, and the work since on disinformation, alternative media, and political mobilization, sits at that intersection: computational methods in service of social science questions, with attention to contexts where data access and prior scholarship are scarce.

The engineering background still shows up when it needs to, but it now serves research rather than the other way around.
